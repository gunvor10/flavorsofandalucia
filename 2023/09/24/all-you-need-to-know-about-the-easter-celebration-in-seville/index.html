All you need to know about the Easter celebration in Seville

Gunvor Guttormsen 24 September 2023 8 min read

With spring in Seville, the city wakes up. It smells of azahar, the bougainvillea lights the streets pink, and the terraces fill.

This is also the time of the two most important celebrations in the Sevillian year: Semana Santa and the Feria de Sevilla. La Feria is a week-long party of flamenco dresses, dancing and cold sherry. Semana Santa is the solemn one — great sculptures of the Passion and of the Virgin carried through the streets on processions that often run more than twelve hours. The whole city takes part, and it lasts over a week.

It is the single most impressive thing to witness in Seville, and also the most confusing if nobody explains it. Here is what is actually happening.

In short

  • —58 processions run between Palm Sunday and Easter Sunday, from 106 churches across the city.
  • —Around 50,000 people take part in robes; some walk barefoot.
  • —A paso can weigh up to a tonne, carried by 20 to 40 costaleros underneath it.
  • —La Madrugá, the night between Maundy Thursday and Good Friday, is the most important night of the week.

01

What exactly is Semana Santa?

From Palm Sunday to Easter Sunday, processions take over the streets of Spain, and locals spend all year preparing for them. It is celebrated across the country, and many trace it back as far as the twelfth century. Seville has been marking it since at least the sixteenth.

The huge sculptures you see — the pasos — originally served an educational purpose, setting out scenes from scripture for those who could not read them. Around 50,000 people walk in traditional robes across 58 organised processions, and the costaleros who carry the pasos, along with others in the procession, often go barefoot as an act of penance.

Most processions set out in the afternoon or evening and continue well into the night. Each brotherhood leaves from its own church and follows an established path onto the carrera oficial, the official route, which begins on Calle Campana and ends once the procession has passed through the cathedral. From there each one finds its way home by a different route.

02

Hermandad

Each of Seville's 106 churches has a brotherhood, an hermandad, attached to it. The brotherhood runs the social life of the parish and, above all, organises its Semana Santa procession. Belonging to one signals a real devotion to the tradition, and in most cases to the faith. Membership is usually inherited, passed down through the generations.

03

El paso

The pasos are the great platforms that make up the procession, each an artistic representation of part of the Passion. Some of the figures are more than three hundred years old. Once they leave the church they move slowly through the narrow streets towards the cathedral, and a procession can be out in the city for twelve to fourteen hours — carried by the same costaleros the whole way.

Some figures over 300 years old12–14 hours in the street

04

Los costaleros

The costaleros are the men who carry the paso. The name comes from el costal, the padded cloth they wear on the head and neck to take the weight as they walk. They are members of the brotherhood, and they train all year, including through the heat of August.

There are between twenty and forty costaleros under each paso, and a paso can weigh up to a tonne. Every Easter a special ward opens at the hospital to treat their injuries. On the longest processions they rotate along the route to rest. In Seville the costaleros walk underneath the paso; in other regions they walk beside it.

05

Los nazarenos

The other figures in the procession are the nazarenos, and their dress is the part visitors find most startling. The robe passes without much comment; the pointed hood, the capirote, resembles a garment worn by the Ku Klux Klan. There is no connection whatsoever between the two, and the Sevillian tradition long predates it.

The dress exists so that a penitent can repent without revealing who they are — the hood leaves only the eyes visible. Some of the larger processions carry up to 3,000 nazarenos. Because the effect can be unsettling, many of them hand out sweets to children along the route to show they are friendly.

Up to 3,000 in one processionHoods preserve anonymity

06

The music

Music is central to the processions. Originally funeral marches were played; today there is a repertoire written specifically for Semana Santa, much of it tuned to the rhythm the costaleros keep as they move the paso. Cornets and drums are the usual instruments. The bands are independent and are engaged by the individual brotherhoods.

07

La mantilla

The headdress women wear on Maundy Thursday and Good Friday: a lace veil worn over a tall comb, the peineta, covering the back of the head. Getting it to sit correctly means weaving the hair around the comb, which is difficult enough that most women have it done professionally.

08

La saeta

The saeta is a religious song delivered at particular moments as a procession passes, sung a cappella from a balcony or from the crowd. Being asked to sing one is an honour, and only the best local singers are given the chance.

For many people it is the high point of the week. The paso halts, the whole street falls completely silent, and one voice carries over it. If you witness one, you will remember it long after the rest.

09

La Madrugá

The most significant night of the week is the one between Maundy Thursday and Good Friday. The name comes from madrugada, the small hours. The pasos set out around midnight and move through the city until midday or later.

The streets are full all night and people follow the processions step by step. Among those out are La Macarena, La Esperanza de Triana and El Silencio, considered some of the largest and most important of the whole of Semana Santa. If you only see one night, see this one — and expect not to sleep.

How to watch it

The dates move with Easter, so check the year before you book anything. Accommodation in the centre is scarce and expensive that week, and much of the old town closes to traffic — expect to walk everywhere, and expect a procession to block your route at some point.

The official programme lists every brotherhood, its route and its timings, and knowing when a paso reaches a given corner is the difference between an hour of waiting and being in the right place. Some of the best moments happen away from the official route, in the narrow streets near a brotherhood's own church, where the paso passes within arm's reach.

Practical advice

  • —Book accommodation months ahead. Semana Santa fills the city entirely.
  • —Do not plan to drive or take taxis in the centre. Processions close streets without much warning.
  • —Dress respectfully in the churches, and stay quiet when a paso passes or a saeta is sung.
  • —Prams and large bags are a real problem in the crowds on the narrow streets.
  • —Eat early. Restaurants near the routes fill up and service stops when a procession passes.
  • —If you want to sleep, La Madrugá is not the night to try.
